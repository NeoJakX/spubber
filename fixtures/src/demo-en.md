# Reading without moving your eyes

When you read a normal page, your eyes do not glide across the text. They jump. Each jump is called a *saccade*, and between jumps the eye stops for a fraction of a second to fixate on a word. Most of your reading time goes into those jumps and stops, not into understanding.

Spubber removes the jumps. Instead of taking your eyes to the words, it brings the words to your eyes: they appear one at a time, always in the same place, at the pace you choose. This technique is known as rapid serial visual presentation, or RSVP.

Look at the coloured letter. It is the optimal recognition point: the spot in each word where your eye identifies it with the least effort. Spubber always places that letter on the same axis, so there is nothing to search for. You only have to look.

# How to use it

There are two ways to read. In **hold mode**, the text advances while you keep the screen or the space bar pressed, and it stops as soon as you let go. It is ideal for getting started because you are always in control. In **tap mode**, one tap starts reading and another one pauses it.

When you stop, Spubber shows you the whole paragraph with the current word highlighted, so you get the context back in a second. Tap any word in that paragraph and reading will continue from there.

Speed is measured in words per minute. An adult reads about 250 on paper. Start a little above your usual pace, for example at 300, and raise it in steps of 25 when you feel comfortable. Spubber does not treat every word the same: it pauses a little longer at commas, longer still at the end of each sentence, and a bit more when a paragraph changes. Long words and numbers also get an extra moment.

On a computer you can use the keyboard: the space bar to read, the left and right arrows to move word by word, the up and down arrows to change the speed, and the B key to save a bookmark.

# Training tips

Read in short sessions. Ten focused minutes are worth more than an hour with your mind somewhere else. Find a quiet spot and put your phone away unless you are reading on it.

Do not vocalise. Many people repeat every word in their head, as if reading aloud. At high speeds that inner voice cannot keep up, and that is fine: let it fall behind and trust what you see.

If you lose the thread, lower the speed by fifty words and climb back up slowly. Understanding always matters more than racing.

# A text to practise with

The lighthouse library had only two shelves, but nobody in the village had ever managed to read them all. The keeper had been filling them for thirty years with books that arrived on the supply boats: navigation manuals, novels without covers, handwritten cookbooks, a Latin dictionary that was missing every word beginning with the letter J.

Marta climbed up for the first time one November afternoon, when the wind kept the boats in harbour. She counted the steps of the spiral staircase, one hundred and twelve, and found the keeper polishing the lens with a woollen cloth.

"You can take one," he said without turning around. "But you have to bring it back before the moon changes."

She chose the thinnest one she could find, thinking that would give her enough time. It was a book about tides. She read it twice that week and a third time aloud to her little brother, who fell asleep on page eleven.

When she climbed up again, the moon had changed. The keeper took the book, put it back in its exact place and nodded towards the bottom shelf.

"Now a thicker one."

That was how Marta learned to read fast. Not because anybody taught her, but because there were too many books and too few moons.
